01 — The diagnostic

On a log axis, exponential growth is a straight line

This is the single chart that settles the question. Debt is plotted on a logarithmic vertical axis, where equal distances mean equal percentage change. The actual series is close to a straight line for fifty years — the signature of constant compound growth. The level-space polynomials (quadratic, cubic) that look impressive on a linear axis are visibly wrong here: they must go negative or near-zero early in the sample to bend hard enough at the end.

02 — Ranking

Model comparison

All models are scored on the same footing: residuals in log space, which is the statistically honest choice when errors scale with the level (a $200B miss in 1965 is a catastrophe; in 2025 it is a rounding error). MAPE is mean absolute percentage error in-sample. AICc rewards fit and penalises parameters — lower is better. Out-of-sample refits each model on 1955–2010 only and scores its predictions for 2011–2025, which is the test that matters for projection.

Read this carefully: the flexible models win in-sample and lose out-of-sample. The 3-segment exponential has the best AICc by a wide margin, but when it is fitted blind to 1955–2010 it puts 2025 debt at against an actual $37.6T. The plain 2-parameter exponential — worse in-sample — was the most accurate forecaster of the models tested. That is the bias-variance trade-off in one table.

03 — Where each model breaks

Percentage error over time

Residuals as a percentage of actual debt. A good model's errors look like noise around zero; a mis-specified model's errors are long slow arcs. The polynomials are off the scale for most of the sample — that is why they are clipped here.

04 — Why the breakpoints are real

Three growth regimes, not one

The rolling 10-year compound growth rate makes the regimes visible without any model at all. Debt grew about 2% a year through the post-war period — slower than the economy, which is why the debt burden shrank until 1974. It then accelerated to a 12–13% peak spanning the 1970s inflation and the 1980s deficits, and has run near 7–8% ever since, with the 2008 and 2020 shocks appearing as bumps rather than a permanent change of slope.

05 — Projection

Where the curve goes by 2030

The forward model is the log-linear fit to the post-2008 regime, the window whose residual spread is small enough to make an interval meaningful (typical error under 5%). The shaded band is a 95% prediction interval from that fit — it is statistical uncertainty in the trend only, and it emphatically does not price in a war, a recession, or a fiscal package.

Every exponential-family fit lands 2030 between roughly $45T and $60T, clustering near $51–54T. For scale, the same models put debt through $40T during FY2026 and $45T around FY2028. A purely linear extension of the last decade's dollar-increments — debt has risen about $2.2T a year since 2022 — gives the low end, near $45T.

06 — The denominator

Debt against the economy that services it

The dollar total is the wrong unit for almost every question people ask about debt. What matters is debt relative to income. Nominal GDP has itself grown exponentially — about 4.4% a year since 1990 — so the ratio is a race between two exponentials, and it is the difference of the two rates that determines the trajectory. At roughly 7% debt growth against 4.4% GDP growth, the ratio keeps climbing at about 2.5 points of GDP a year.

The ratio bottomed at 30.6% in 1974 and stands at 122.4% for FY2025 — a fourfold rise in fifty years. Extending both exponentials puts it near 135–145% by 2030. Note this is gross debt, which includes about $7T the government owes itself (mostly Social Security and Medicare trust funds); debt held by the public is the smaller figure usually quoted in CBO projections.

07 — Method & caveats

What this analysis can and cannot tell you

Fitting. Linear-in-parameters models are solved by ordinary least squares via normal equations. Multiplicative models are fitted by OLS on ln(D), which is the maximum-likelihood estimator under proportional errors. Breakpoints, the singularity time tc, and the logistic ceiling K are found by exhaustive grid search over the remaining nonlinear parameter, minimising log-space SSE.

Scoring. AICc uses the log-space residual sum of squares with a small-sample correction; k counts every fitted quantity including grid-searched breakpoints, so the flexible models are penalised for the search.

Curve fitting is not economics. None of these models contains a deficit, an interest rate, or a Congress. They are descriptions of a trajectory, and they hold only while the process that generated it holds. The 1972 and 1990 breakpoints are precisely the evidence that the process does change.

The singularity model is a red flag, not a forecast. A finite-time-blow-up form fits this data well enough to rank mid-table, which is a reminder that within-sample fit cannot distinguish "fast exponential" from "heading for a wall" — the two diverge only outside the data.

Interest is the feedback term. The one mechanism that makes pure exponentiality self-fulfilling is compounding interest on the stock itself, now the fastest-growing line in the federal budget.
